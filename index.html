The Problem

Most frontend problems don't start as big mistakes. They start as small decisions nobody questions.

  1. [01]

    It works. But does it work everywhere?

    A website can look perfect on your screen and still fail on someone else's. If it only works where you tested it, is it really finished?

  2. [02]

    Why does a small change break something else?

    If fixing one thing creates another problem, the issue might not be the change. It might be how everything is connected.

  3. [03]

    If users have to think too much, what went wrong?

    A good interface shouldn't make people guess what to click, where to go, or what happens next. The design should make the next step obvious.

  4. [04]

    What's the point of a beautiful website that feels slow?

    Users don't experience your design file. They experience every delay, every jump, and every frustrating interaction.

The Real Issue

The problem isn't always what you see. It's what you overlooked.

Good frontend development means questioning the small decisions before they become big problems. That's where better experiences begin.

My story

I’m a Frontend Web Developer in the making who turns logic into clean, usable design. I’m a 4th-semester computerscience student, and the way I learn is simple: I build things.

I wrote my first line of code at 19 on freeCodeCamp, and for a while it was just exercises and syntax. Then I decided to build something for a real person. A friend’s birthday was coming up, so instead of sending a text, I built him a small website. When he opened it, he went quiet for a second, then grinned and said, “You made this? For me?” That moment changed how I saw code. It stopped being homework and became a way to make someone’s day.

That project also taught me my first real lesson: reading about code and shipping it are two very different skills. The page looked perfect on my laptop and fell apart on his phone. One CSS rule overrode another, an image broke the layout, and a button did nothing because of a typo in a JavaScript variable name. I spent hours chasing each bug, and I learned more from that one afternoon than from a week of tutorials.

Today I’m working toward becoming a full-stack developer. My mindset is logic first, design second, before I write code, I ask what problem I’m solving and how the pieces connect. I’d rather understand why something works than copy something that happens to work. If I can’t explain a piece of code in my own words, I’m not done with it yet.

Debugging is also where I found something I didn’t expect: I stay calm when things break. When some classmates were building an Adidas shoe store website, bugs kept piling up and nobody could figure out why. More than once I heard, “We can’t do this,” and they were ready to give up. I sat down with them, read through their code line by line, and helped trace each problem back to its cause. Each time a bug disappeared, their energy came back. I realized that debugging isn’t just a technical skill; it’s about keeping people’s confidence alive while the code is broken.

What I care about most is clarity, clear code, clear layouts, and clear communication. I’d much rather build something small that works well than something massive that falls apart.

I’m now looking for internships and remote opportunities for three reasons. I love building new things and learning by shipping them. I enjoy solving people’s problems and giving them a design that feels easy to use. And I want to grow into someone who can work independently and build a career on work I’m proud of. Away from the keyboard, I’m usually out with friends, or back at my computer turning small ideas into code. Both keep me grounded, and I bring the same attitude to each: try it, see what happens, and learn from it.
